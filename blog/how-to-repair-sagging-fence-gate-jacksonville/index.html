How to Repair a Sagging Fence Gate in Jacksonville

August 18, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

If your wood fence gate has started dragging on the ground, catching on the latch, or hanging at a visible angle, you are not alone. It is one of the most common repair calls we get around Jacksonville, and it is usually fixable in an afternoon without replacing the whole gate. The culprits are almost always the same three things: a hinge post loosened in our sandy soil, hardware corroded by salt air, or a frame never braced against racking. This guide covers diagnosing the cause, choosing between an anti-sag cable kit and a diagonal brace, resetting a wobbly hinge post, and picking hardware that will hold up here.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

Repairing an existing gate, meaning tightening hinges, replacing hardware, adding a brace or turnbuckle, resetting the same post in the same hole, or swapping rotted pickets, is routine maintenance and does not typically require a permit from the City of Jacksonville. Permitting comes into play with new fence construction: building a new fence or replacing most of an existing one generally requires a Duval County permit, plus height limits and setback rules that depend on the yard the fence sits in. If your gate is part of a fence enclosing a pool, Florida's pool safety law requires it to be self-closing and self-latching, with the latch out of a small child's reach, whether repairing or replacing. Permit thresholds and pool barrier specifics are set and enforced locally, so confirm your situation with the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division before you start, especially if the repair grows into more than fixing what is already there.

Tools and Materials

  • Tape measure, level, speed square
  • Drill/driver with exterior-rated bits
  • Socket wrench set for lag bolts
  • Post level or a helper to plumb the post
  • Shovel or post hole digger, if resetting a post
  • Fast-setting concrete mix, if the footing has failed
  • Stainless steel or hot-dip galvanized hinges, screws, and lag bolts
  • Anti-sag cable and turnbuckle kit, or pressure-treated 2x4 for a diagonal brace
  • Exterior wood glue and deck screws for frame joints
  • Wood filler or epoxy consolidant for minor rot

Step-by-Step

  1. Diagnose the problem. Open and close the gate slowly and watch where the movement comes from. If the whole post leans when pushed, the footing has failed. If the post is solid but the frame has gone parallelogram-shaped, it has racked and needs bracing, not a new post.
  1. Check and tighten hardware first. Loose hinge screws are the single most common cause of a dragging gate. Remove rusted screws, fill stripped holes with wood glue and toothpicks or a golf tee, let dry, and re-drive with longer stainless screws.
  1. Reset the hinge post if it is leaning. Dig out the base, remove cracked or crumbled concrete, plumb the post, and repour with fast-setting concrete, crowned so water sheds away. Let it cure fully, typically 24 to 48 hours, before rehanging.
  1. Choose your anti-sag fix. A cable kit runs a turnbuckle-tensioned cable corner to corner and is the easiest retrofit on a gate already hung, since you tighten it as the frame settles. A diagonal wood brace, run bottom hinge-side to top latch-side, is more permanent and handles heavier gates and wind better, but means partly disassembling the frame to install.
  1. Rehang, adjust, and seal. Reattach the gate, check that it swings freely, adjust the latch if the post shifted, and seal any new cuts or joints against moisture.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Jacksonville's coastal air is hard on fence hardware. Near the beaches or the Intracoastal, standard zinc-plated screws and hinges can start rusting within a year, which is why stainless or true hot-dip galvanized hardware is worth the cost metro-wide, not just near the water. Humidity also swells untreated wood in the wet season and lets it shrink back in drier stretches, and that movement loosens hinge screws and opens frame joints over time. Sandy soil, common across Duval County, does not hold a post as firmly as denser clay, so a shallow footing is a frequent cause of a leaning post. Check the post base and bottom rail for termite damage or rot, where moisture sits longest. If rebuilding rather than repairing, size bracing for hurricane-season wind, since a gate without a brace or cable is one of the first things to rack or blow off in a storm.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I use an anti-sag cable kit or a diagonal brace?
A cable kit is faster to install on a gate already hung and lets you re-tension it as the frame settles, a good choice for lighter gates or a quick fix. A diagonal wood brace is stiffer, holds up better under heavier gates or strong wind, and needs no periodic tightening, but typically requires taking part of the frame apart to fit correctly. For a standard gate either works; for a heavier or wider one, the brace tends to last longer.
Why does my gate keep sagging again after I fix it?
The most common repeat cause here is a hinge post that was never firmly set, often because the original footing was too shallow or the concrete did not fully surround the base. Sandy soil makes this worse, since it does not grip a marginal footing the way denser soil would. If the gate sags again within a season or two after a hardware fix, the post is usually the real problem.
Do I need a permit just to fix my gate?
No. Repairing an existing gate, meaning tightening hardware, adding a brace or cable, resetting the same post, or replacing damaged boards, is generally maintenance and does not require a permit. A permit becomes relevant if you are building a new fence or replacing most of it, or if the work affects a required pool barrier. Confirm with the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division if unsure which category your project falls into.
