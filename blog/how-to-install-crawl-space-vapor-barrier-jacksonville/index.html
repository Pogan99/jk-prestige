How to Install a Crawl Space Vapor Barrier Under a Raised Jacksonville House

August 18, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

Most new construction in Duval County is slab on grade, so if you are reading this you probably own one of the older raised houses in Springfield, Riverside, Avondale, Murray Hill, or a beach cottage near Atlantic or Neptune Beach. Those homes sit on piers with an open crawl space underneath, and bare Jacksonville dirt under a house pushes moisture upward constantly. A ground vapor barrier is the cheapest, most effective way to cut that moisture load before it turns into musty floors, rusted ductwork, or rotted joists. This guide covers prepping the space, choosing between 6 mil poly and a reinforced liner, running the sheet up the piers and foundation walls, and deciding whether to stay vented or move toward encapsulation.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

Laying a ground vapor barrier in an existing vented crawl space is generally treated as maintenance in Duval County and typically does not require a permit on its own. That changes once you alter how the crawl space breathes or performs. Full encapsulation that closes off the foundation vents, adds a dehumidifier, or introduces conditioned air is a different assembly, and it usually brings mechanical and electrical work into the project, work that has to be done or permitted by a licensed trade. If any part of the crawl space sits below the base flood elevation for your lot, common in low-lying pockets of Jacksonville near the river and the beaches, flood zone rules can restrict what you are allowed to enclose or condition down there. Before buying materials, call the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division and describe exactly what you are planning, a simple ground liner versus a sealed and mechanically dried crawl space, so you know which one you are actually doing.

Tools and Materials

  • 6 mil polyethylene sheeting, or a reinforced cross-laminated liner (12 to 20 mil) for rough terrain or regular foot traffic
  • Seam tape rated for crawl space vapor barriers, and a termite inspection gap strip kept separate from the barrier
  • Construction adhesive and masonry fasteners or a termination bar for anchoring the barrier at piers and the foundation wall
  • Utility knife, tape measure, chalk line
  • Headlamp, knee pads, coveralls, and a respirator rated for mold and dust
  • Wet/dry shop vac for standing water, rake for debris, contractor bags for hauling it out
  • A helper, since dragging sheeting through a two-foot crawl space alone is slow going

Step-by-Step

  1. Get eyes on the whole space first. Crawl the perimeter with a light and check for standing water, sewage odor, active leaks, or mold on the joists. A confined, low-oxygen space with wet ground, exposed wiring, and the occasional snake or spider is not a casual Saturday project, and if you find standing water, sewage, or mold growth on the wood, stop and call a professional. That is a moisture or plumbing problem to fix first, not something to cover up with a liner.
  2. Clear debris and grade rough spots. Rake out old insulation, scrap wood, trash, and any degraded prior liner, then fill deep low spots so the liner is not draped over sharp rocks or roots.
  3. Choose your liner. Plain 6 mil poly works fine in a crawl space nobody enters after installation day. If a technician will be back for HVAC or plumbing work, or the ground is rocky, spend the extra money on a reinforced 12 to 20 mil liner.
  4. Roll, overlap, and tape. Work across the space, overlapping each sheet at least 12 inches at the seams, and tape every seam with tape rated for the material, not general purpose duct tape.
  5. Run it up the piers and walls. Wrap the barrier up each masonry pier and up the foundation wall at least 6 to 8 inches, and secure it with adhesive and a termination bar so it does not slump back down. This is the step most DIY jobs skip, and it is the reason vapor still finds its way up around the piers even after the ground is covered.
  6. Leave the termite inspection gap. Keep the top edge of the barrier a few inches short of any wood framing, and never let it touch the sill plate or joists. Pest control needs a clear visual strip along the foundation to spot mud tubes.
  7. Seal penetrations and double-check seams. Cut the barrier tight around piers, plumbing stacks, and HVAC supports and tape those cuts closed, then walk the space a second time and press down every seam and pier wrap to confirm the tape is holding.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Northeast Florida sits on sandy soil with a high water table, so ground moisture under a house is a given, not an occasional problem. In summer, outside dew points regularly climb into the 70s, and when that humid air drifts into a crawl space cooled by shaded, damp ground, it condenses on ductwork, subfloor, and framing the same way a cold glass sweats on a porch table. That is why sealing the vents without a real moisture plan, whether that is a properly lapped barrier, drainage, or a dehumidifier, can trap humidity inside and make rot and mold worse instead of better. Keep the barrier off any wood and hold that termite inspection gap, since subterranean termites are active year round here and a hidden liner against the sill plate is exactly the kind of cover they like. If your lot has flooded before or sits in a mapped flood zone, plan the work with hurricane season storm surge in mind, since a sealed crawl space with nowhere for floodwater to drain can hold water against the framing longer than an open, vented one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to remove the old vapor barrier before installing a new one?
Yes, if the old liner is torn, moldy, or wet underneath. Leaving a degraded barrier in place traps moisture between the two layers and defeats the purpose of the new one. If the old barrier is intact and dry, some installers lap new material over it, but pulling it out and starting clean is the safer bet in a humid climate like Jacksonville's.
Can I seal my crawl space vents myself, or does that require a permit?
Closing off vents is part of full encapsulation, and that usually brings mechanical or electrical components, a dehumidifier, conditioned air, or fans, into the project. That work generally needs a permit and a licensed trade in Duval County. Confirm the specifics with the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division first, since unpermitted work can complicate a future home sale or insurance claim.
How long does a 6 mil vapor barrier actually last in a Florida crawl space?
Plain 6 mil poly typically holds up for several years in a stable, low-traffic crawl space, but Jacksonville's humidity, sandy ground, and occasional standing water shorten its working life compared to a drier climate. If anyone will be back under the house for repairs, or rocks are present, a reinforced 12 to 20 mil liner is worth the extra cost because it resists punctures and tape failure far longer.
