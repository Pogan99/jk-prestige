How to Replace Interior Door Casing and Trim in a Jacksonville Home

August 18, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

Interior door casing takes more abuse than almost any other trim in the house. Kids swing on it, vacuums bang into it, humidity opens the miters, and decades of paint bury the profile. Replacing it is an approachable DIY project, but it is easy to turn a one-hour job into a drywall repair if you pull the old casing the wrong way. Here is how to remove it cleanly, set a reveal that looks intentional, choose between mitered and butted craftsman joints, and match trim in an older Riverside or Springfield house where the box-store profile will not match what is already on the wall.

Every step below assumes standard swing doors and typical wood or MDF casing, not the specialty profiles found on some historic openings.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

Replacing interior door casing and trim is finish work, and finish work does not require a permit in Duval County. You are not touching structure, electrical, or plumbing, so you can pull the old casing, reset the reveal, and install new trim without a trip to the counter. That changes if you are widening or reframing the door opening, moving the jamb, or removing a wall to change the room layout. That crosses into structural work, and the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division does require a permit for it.

If your house sits in Riverside, Avondale, Springfield, or another local historic district, interior trim work is generally outside Historic Preservation Commission review, since that review focuses on what is visible from the street. Exterior door and window trim is a different story and can trigger review, so do not assume interior rules carry over to the porch.

One more thing if your Springfield or Riverside house predates 1978: EPA lead-safe work practice rules apply whenever you disturb painted surfaces in a pre-1978 home, including prying off old casing and sanding the jamb. That means containment and cleanup, not a permit, but it is a real requirement. When in doubt, call the Building Inspection Division and confirm what your project needs.

Tools and Materials

  • Flat pry bar and a small putty knife
  • Utility knife, nail set, and end nippers
  • Miter saw
  • Tape measure, combination square, pencil
  • Finish nailer or hammer and finish nails
  • Wood filler or paintable caulk
  • New casing stock, matched profile if possible
  • 3/16-inch or 1/4-inch scrap for setting the reveal
  • Primer and paint, or stain and clear coat

Step-by-Step

  1. Score the paint line where the casing meets the wall and the jamb. Paint acts like glue, and skipping this is the top reason drywall paper tears when the casing comes off.
  1. Pry from the middle out. Protect the wall with a putty knife, work a flat pry bar behind the casing near the center of the longest leg, and pull gently. Move to the next nail rather than levering the whole piece from one spot. Pull nails through the back with end nippers once loose.
  1. Clear the jamb and wall. Pull remaining nails through from the back, or clip them flush. Patch any torn drywall corners before moving on.
  1. Set your reveal, the small setback, typically 3/16 to 1/4 inch, between the jamb edge and the casing face. Cut a scrap block to that width, use it as a spacer, and trace a pencil line around the whole jamb so every piece lines up the same way.
  1. Cut and dry-fit the head casing first, then the two side legs. For mitered craftsman casing, cut 45-degree angles at both ends of the head and matching 45s on top of each leg. For butted craftsman casing, common in Prairie and Craftsman-style homes, cut the head square and let the legs butt into it at 90 degrees, more forgiving if the walls are not perfectly square.
  1. Nail to the reveal line, working top down, into the jamb along the inner edge and into framing along the outer edge. Set nails below the surface.
  1. Fill, sand, and finish. Fill nail holes and joints, sand lightly, then prime and paint or stain to match.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Northeast Florida's humidity swings hard between a wet summer and a drier winter, and solid wood trim moves with it. A miter cut tight in July can open a hairline gap by January as the wood shrinks. Acclimate new casing stock inside the room for a few days before cutting so it matches your house's humidity, not the truck it rode in on.

MDF casing, common locally because it takes paint smoothly and resists warping, has one real weakness: standing water. If it sits in a humid garage or gets rained on before installation, it swells at the cut edges and never fully recovers. Store it flat, indoors, off the concrete floor.

In Riverside, Springfield, and other older Jacksonville neighborhoods, original casing is often old-growth heart pine, denser and more rot and termite resistant than modern fast-growth pine, worth knowing before you toss it, since salvaged pieces can sometimes be reused or matched by a millwork shop. Termites stay active in Duval County year-round, so soft, hollow, or mud-tubed old casing is a signal to get the area inspected, not just replaced. Keep the AC running normally while you work; cutting it lets humidity climb, which is what causes fresh miters to open back up within months.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to remove the door and hinges before replacing the casing?
No. The casing sits on the wall side of the jamb, separate from the door and hinges. You can leave the door hung and work around it, though pulling it temporarily makes the pry bar work easier along the hinge-side leg.
What reveal size should I use to match the rest of my house?
Check an untouched door in another room first. Most Jacksonville homes built from the mid-20th century on use a 1/4-inch reveal, while some older craftsman-era houses in Riverside and Springfield used a tighter 3/16 inch. Matching what is already established in the house matters more than any single "correct" number.
Can I mix mitered and butted casing styles in the same house?
You can, but it looks inconsistent if both styles are visible from the same sightline, such as two doors off one hallway. Using butted casing in one wing and mitered casing in another is fine where the transition falls somewhere natural, but within one room or hallway, pick one joint style and stay with it.
