How to Replace Rotted Deck Stair Stringers in Jacksonville

August 18, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

If a deck stair bounces or flexes underfoot, there is a good chance the stringers, the diagonal boards that carry every step, have started to rot. In Jacksonville's climate, ground contact moisture and long humid stretches can take a stringer from solid to soft faster than homeowners expect. Replacing stair stringers is exacting work because stairs are a life safety item. Every riser has to match the one next to it almost exactly, or someone's foot will catch on the way down. This guide covers what a Jacksonville homeowner needs to know before pulling out the old stringers, including when the city wants a permit, how to lay out even risers, and which hardware holds up near the coast.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

A straight like-for-like repair, swapping a rotted stringer for a new one of the same size and location, is often treated as maintenance in many jurisdictions. But stair and deck framing is structural, and Duval County and the City of Jacksonville commonly require a permit and inspection for structural deck and stair work, especially anything attached to the house or elevated off the ground. Call the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division before you cut a single board and describe exactly what you are replacing, since the answer depends on the deck's height, its attachment to the house, and how much of the structure you are touching. Rental properties can carry extra inspection requirements, and a deck in a flood zone, which covers property near the St. Johns River and the Intracoastal, can have extra rules about work below the base flood elevation. Do not assume a small stair repair is exempt. A five minute phone call is cheaper than a stop work order.

Tools and Materials

  • Tape measure, framing square, and speed square
  • Stair gauge clips or a stringer layout tool
  • Circular saw and a handsaw or jigsaw for the seat cuts
  • Level and string line for checking the landing
  • Pressure treated 2x12 lumber rated for ground contact
  • Hot dip galvanized or stainless structural screws rated for treated lumber
  • Stair stringer connectors or hurricane style hangers
  • Concrete mix or paver base material for the landing pad
  • Ledger hardware if the stringers attach to a ledger board

Step-by-Step

  1. Measure total rise and total run. Measure from the top decking surface down to the finished grade or landing pad for your total rise. Measure the horizontal distance the stairs will cover for your total run.
  2. Calculate the number of risers. Divide the total rise by a target riser height in the range typically used for exterior stairs, then round to a whole number. Divide the total rise by that whole number to get your exact riser height, since every riser in the run has to match. Uneven risers, even by a fraction of an inch, are a common cause of trips on outdoor stairs.
  3. Lay out and cut the stringers. Set stair gauge clips on your framing square to the riser height and tread depth, then walk the square down a piece of pressure treated 2x12 to mark each step. Cut one stringer first, test fit it against the deck rim and landing, then use it as a template so the rest match exactly.
  4. Attach at the top. Use stringer hangers or approved connectors at the ledger or rim joist rather than toenailing, which loosens over time.
  5. Set the landing pad. The bottom of the stringers needs a solid, level, properly draining surface, a concrete pad or compacted paver base. A stringer resting on soil or mulch will rot again quickly.
  6. Check code minimums before you finish. Codes set a maximum riser height, a minimum tread depth, and a graspable handrail requirement once a stair passes a small number of steps. Do not guess at these numbers. Confirm current figures with the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division or your inspector before finalizing cuts.
  7. Install treads, risers, and rail. Attach treads with corrosion resistant fasteners, add a handrail if required, and walk test the assembly before calling it done.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Northeast Florida is hard on exterior wood. The point where a stringer meets soil or a low landing pad is the single most common failure spot, because moisture wicks into end grain and stays there in our humidity, inviting rot before the rest of the board shows damage. Subterranean termites are active throughout Duval County, and a soft, damaged stringer is an easy target, so it is worth a quick look for mud tubes or hollow sounding wood while the old stair is torn apart. Pressure treated lumber also moves as it dries, shrinking and sometimes twisting after installation, which is one more reason to use screws rather than smooth nails, since screws hold through that movement. Near the beaches or the Intracoastal, salt air corrodes standard fasteners and hangers far faster than inland, so hot dip galvanized or stainless hardware rated for treated lumber is not optional there, it is the difference between hardware that lasts and hardware that fails quietly underneath the stair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to replace just the stair stringers on my deck?
It depends on the scope and how the city classifies the work. Structural deck and stair repairs are commonly permitted and inspected in Jacksonville, and elevated or house attached decks are treated seriously. Call the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division and describe your specific repair before you start.
How do I know if my stair risers are even enough?
Measure the height of each individual step with a tape measure or level, not just by eye. Even a small inconsistency between risers is a common trip hazard, and building codes require riser heights to stay within a narrow, uniform range. If you find a variance, recut the affected stringer rather than shimming it.
What kind of wood and hardware should I use for a stair stringer in Florida?
Use pressure treated lumber rated for ground contact anywhere the stringer is near soil, a pad, or persistent moisture, paired with hot dip galvanized or stainless steel fasteners and connectors rated for treated lumber. Standard hardware corrodes faster here than in drier climates, especially near the coast, and undersized or mismatched fasteners are a frequent cause of early stair failure.
