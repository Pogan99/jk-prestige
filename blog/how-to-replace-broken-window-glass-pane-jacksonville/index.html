How to Replace a Broken Window Glass Pane in Jacksonville

August 8, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

A cracked or shattered window pane is one of those repairs that seems small until you're standing in front of it with a putty knife, wondering whether you're about to make things worse. In Jacksonville, the job itself is straightforward for most single-pane and insulated-glass residential windows, but the details that trip people up are usually local: what kind of glass you have, whether it's just the pane or the whole unit, and how our humidity and storm exposure affect the materials you choose. Here's how to handle a standard single or double-pane replacement, and when to call in a pro instead.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

For most homeowners, replacing just the glass pane in an existing sash or frame does not require a permit from the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division. This counts as routine repair and maintenance, similar to replacing a broken pane in a door, and it doesn't change the size, structure, or rating of the window opening.

That changes once the scope grows. Replacing the entire window unit, frame included, alters a building envelope opening, and that generally does require a permit under the Florida Building Code. This matters more in Jacksonville than in inland Florida because much of Duval County, especially near the St. Johns River and the coast, falls within Florida's wind-borne debris region, where full window replacements typically need to meet impact resistance or opening protection requirements. That's a different job than swapping out a cracked pane.

A permit is also more likely for insurance-covered storm damage repair, an impact-glass upgrade, or a window in a historic district subject to design review. Because thresholds and fees change, confirm your situation with the Building Inspection Division before starting work.

Tools and Materials

  • Replacement glass pane, cut to size (or an insulated glass unit if your window is double-pane)
  • Safety glasses and work gloves
  • Putty knife or 5-in-1 painter's tool
  • Heat gun (optional, for stubborn old glazing putty)
  • Glazier's points or clips, depending on window type
  • Glazing putty or compound (for wood sash windows)
  • Rubber or vinyl glazing gasket (for aluminum or vinyl frame windows)
  • Caulk gun and exterior-grade silicone caulk
  • Wire brush or scraper for cleaning the sash channel
  • Measuring tape
  • Drop cloth and a shop vac for glass debris
  • Painter's tape

Step-by-Step

  1. Confirm the window type. Identify whether you have a single-pane wood sash, an aluminum or vinyl frame with a rubber gasket, or a double-pane insulated glass unit. This determines whether you'll use glazing putty or a gasket system.
  1. Remove the broken glass safely. Wearing gloves and safety glasses, tape an X across the pane to hold shards together, then work pieces loose from the outside in. Dispose of glass in a rigid container, not a trash bag.
  1. Strip out old glazing or gasket material. Use a putty knife, and a heat gun if needed, to clean the sash channel down to bare wood or frame. Remove old glazier's points and any embedded shards.
  1. Take precise measurements. Measure the opening in two directions at multiple points, since older Jacksonville homes often have slightly out-of-square openings from decades of humidity-driven wood movement. Order or cut glass with proper clearance.
  1. Set the new pane. Bed the glass in a thin layer of glazing compound and secure it with glazier's points on wood sash windows, or seat it into the rubber gasket on aluminum or vinyl frames.
  1. Glaze and seal. Apply glazing putty over the points on wood windows, feathering it to a clean bevel. On aluminum or vinyl frames, run a bead of exterior-grade silicone where the gasket meets the frame.
  1. Cure and paint. Let glazing putty cure per the manufacturer's timeline, typically one to two weeks, before painting to match the sash and protect it from moisture.
  1. Test the seal. Check for drafts or water intrusion during the next rain, and confirm the sash still opens, closes, and locks properly.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Jacksonville's humidity, salt-laden coastal air, and intense sun affect both the process and the materials you choose. Glazing putty cures slower in our humidity, so give it extra time before painting and avoid glazing on a rainy day. If your home is near the Intracoastal, the St. Johns River's mouth, or the beaches, salt air accelerates corrosion on aluminum hardware and glazier's points, so stainless points and marine-grade sealants are worth the small upcharge.

Wind load matters too. Even though a single-pane repair usually isn't a permit trigger, homeowners in wind-borne debris areas should still consider laminated or impact-rated glass as an upgrade when a pane needs replacing anyway, especially on windows without shutter protection. And intense UV exposure breaks down standard caulk and glazing compound faster here than in milder climates, so UV-stable, exterior-rated sealants extend the life of the repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to replace just the glass in my window?
In most cases, no. Replacing only the glass pane within an existing sash or frame is treated as routine repair, not permit-required work. Full window unit replacement is different and often does require one, so confirm your specific scope with the Building Inspection Division if you're unsure.
Can I replace a double-pane insulated glass unit myself?
It's possible, but insulated glass units are heavier, more precisely sized, and pricier than single-pane glass, and a poor seal defeats their insulating purpose. Many homeowners have the glass professionally measured and cut, then install it themselves or have a contractor set it for a proper seal.
Should I upgrade to impact-rated glass while I'm already replacing a pane?
If your home is in a wind-borne debris area of Duval County without shutter protection, it can make sense to consider laminated or impact-rated glass while a pane needs replacing anyway, since you're already paying for labor. It costs more upfront but reduces storm risk and can lower insurance premiums, so it's worth pricing out.
