How to Regrout a Bathroom Floor in Jacksonville: Removing Failed Grout the Right Way

August 18, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

Cracked, crumbling, or discolored grout is one of the most common bathroom complaints we hear from Jacksonville homeowners, and it is also one of the more forgiving DIY repairs, as long as the tile underneath is sound. Regrouting done correctly can add years to a floor without a full tear-out. Done over a floor that already has a structural problem, it is a weekend lost to a repair that fails again within months. Here is how to tell the difference, and how to do the job right if your floor qualifies.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

Straight regrouting is maintenance, not construction, and Duval County does not require a permit to remove and replace grout on an existing tile floor. That changes once the scope grows. If you are tearing out the tile and replacing the substrate or the waterproofing membrane underneath, that is a repair to the building assembly and typically needs a permit through the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division. The same is true if the work involves moving plumbing, such as relocating a drain or supply line under the floor. If your home was built before 1978 and you will be disturbing painted trim, baseboards, or a painted subfloor near the work area, lead-safe work practices apply under federal renovation rules, regardless of whether a permit is pulled. Confirm your specific scope with the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division before starting anything beyond simple grout removal and replacement.

Tools and Materials

  • Oscillating multi-tool with a grout removal blade, or a manual grout saw for small areas
  • Rotary grout removal tool for larger floors (rent one for anything over roughly 40 square feet)
  • Shop vacuum with a HEPA filter and a vacuum shroud attached to the grinding tool
  • Respirator rated for silica dust, safety glasses, knee pads, painter's tape
  • Grout float, mixing bucket, margin trowel, sponges, clean water
  • Sanded or unsanded grout matched to joint width and existing color
  • 100% silicone caulk in a matching color for all changes of plane
  • Penetrating grout sealer

Step-by-Step

  1. Diagnose before you grind. Tap across the tile field with a plastic screwdriver handle or small mallet. Solid tile sounds tight and dense; a hollow or drummy sound means it has debonded from the substrate. If several tiles sound hollow, or the same grout line keeps cracking in the same spot no matter how many times it has been patched, that points to substrate movement or a failed bond, not a grout problem. Regrouting over loose or hollow tile is wasted work, since new grout will crack again as the tile flexes. That situation calls for tile replacement, and is worth a contractor's opinion before you buy materials.
  2. Protect the room and control dust. Tape off cabinets, the tub, and the toilet base. Attach a vacuum shroud to your grinding tool and run extraction continuously. Grinding old grout produces fine silica dust, and repeated unprotected exposure is a genuine respiratory hazard, so wear a respirator for the whole job, not just the loud parts.
  3. Remove and clean the joints. Run the oscillating tool or grout saw down the center of each joint to about two-thirds the tile thickness, working side to side without touching the tile edges. Vacuum as you go, then wipe with a damp sponge to clear settled dust, and let the floor dry fully.
  4. Choose sanded or unsanded. Use sanded grout for joints 1/8 inch or wider, since the sand aggregate resists shrinkage. Use unsanded grout for narrower joints or polished stone, where sand could scratch the surface.
  5. Match the color. Bring a chip of the old grout, or a photo taken in natural light, to the tile store rather than guessing from a color card. Grout shifts as it cures and can look different indoors under bathroom lighting than on a store display.
  6. Mix, apply, and shape the joints. Mix small batches to the manufacturer's ratio, avoiding a soupy mix that shrinks and cracks. Pack the float diagonally across the tile at roughly a 45 degree angle, then wipe diagonally with a damp sponge, rinsing often, before a haze forms, working in stages.
  7. Caulk every change of plane. Where floor tile meets the tub, a wall, a curb, or a cabinet base, use silicone caulk, not grout. Rigid grout cannot flex with the movement between two separate building assemblies, and it will crack out within a season. This is one of the most common mistakes in DIY regrout jobs.
  8. Seal and cure. Let the grout cure per the manufacturer's timeline, typically 48 to 72 hours, before applying a penetrating sealer, and keep the floor dry during that window.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Northeast Florida's humidity works against you at nearly every step. High ambient humidity slows grout cure time and can leave color looking inconsistent between sections if you do not work in matched batches and clean at the same intervals throughout. Bathrooms with weak exhaust ventilation are prone to mildew forming in fresh grout lines within weeks if the room is not actively dried out after showers, so confirm your fan works and gets used. Parts of Duval County have moderately hard water, which over time leaves mineral deposits that dull grout color and interfere with sealer performance, so sealing matters more here than in areas with softer water. If your bathroom sits on a slab, moisture migrating up through it can also affect how well new grout bonds, especially in older homes without a modern vapor barrier. In peak summer, heat and humidity shorten your working time before grout skins over, so mix smaller batches than you would in cooler weather.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my grout problem is really a tile problem?
Tap across the entire floor with a hard plastic handle or small mallet and listen for a hollow, drummy sound compared to the solid sound of well-bonded tile. If the hollow spots are scattered or widespread, or the same crack keeps reappearing in the same joint after repeated patching, the tile has likely debonded from the substrate. That is a bonding or movement issue, and new grout will not fix it permanently.
Can I regrout without removing all the old grout first?
No. Grout does not bond reliably to old grout, dust, or soap film, and new material applied over the old layer will delaminate within a short time. The old grout needs to be cut out to roughly two-thirds the tile depth and the joint cleaned before new grout goes in.
Why does my grout keep cracking right where the floor meets the tub?
That joint is a change of plane between two separate building assemblies, the floor and the tub or wall, which move slightly and independently from each other. Grout is rigid and cannot absorb that movement, so it cracks out over time. Those joints should always be filled with 100% silicone caulk instead of grout, which flexes with the movement and holds up far longer.
