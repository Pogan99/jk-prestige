How to Replace Shower Door Seals and the Bottom Sweep in Jacksonville

August 18, 2026 · 6 min read · JK Prestige Constructor Corp

If water is creeping past your glass shower door and pooling on the bathroom floor, the culprit is usually a worn bottom sweep or a hardened side seal, not a failing shower pan. These parts are cheap, sold by the foot at glass and hardware suppliers, and a patient homeowner can swap them out in an afternoon. The trick is identifying the exact profile your door uses, since sweeps and seals are not universal, and knowing when the real problem is the caulk joint at the curb instead of the door. This guide covers both, with an eye toward Jacksonville's humidity, hard water and how an ignored leak quietly works its way into the framing behind the tile.

When You Need a Permit in Jacksonville

Replacing a door sweep, side seal or drip rail is routine maintenance. It does not touch plumbing, structure or waterproofing, so no permit is required in Duval County. The same goes for recaulking a shower curb or the glass-to-tile joint. You cross into permit territory when the work changes the shower pan, moves or replaces water supply or drain lines, alters the waterproofing membrane, or converts a tub to a shower. A shower pan replacement in Jacksonville typically requires a plumbing permit and a shower pan test inspection before the walls close back up, since the city wants to see the pan hold water first. If your project is limited to hardware on the door, you are fine without a permit, but if the leak turns out to be a soft subfloor or a failed pan liner, that is a different job. When in doubt, confirm with the City of Jacksonville Building Inspection Division before you start.

Tools and Materials

  • Replacement vinyl sweep, side seals or drip rail matching your door's profile
  • Utility knife or fine-tooth hacksaw
  • Tape measure
  • Flathead screwdriver or plastic trim tool
  • Rubbing alcohol and a lint-free cloth
  • Razor blade scraper for scale or adhesive residue
  • 100% silicone caulk and caulk gun (if recaulking the curb)
  • Painter's tape for a clean caulk line

Step-by-Step

  1. Identify the profile. Pull the old sweep or seal off the glass and take it, or a clear photo with a ruler beside it, to a glass shop or hardware supplier. Channel widths and wing shapes vary, and the wrong match will pop off within a week.
  1. Measure the glass edge. Measure the height of the panel where the seal actually runs, not the door opening. Replacement strips come long and get trimmed down, so measure twice before cutting.
  1. Remove the old part. Slide a flathead screwdriver or plastic trim tool under the lip and work the old piece off the glass edge. It will usually be stiff, cracked or shrunk from age and heat, which is why it leaks.
  1. Clean the glass edge. Scrape off dried caulk, soap scum or hard water scale with a razor blade held flat against the glass, then wipe the edge with rubbing alcohol. Any residue left behind keeps the new seal from seating fully.
  1. Cut the new piece to length. Use a sharp utility knife or fine-tooth hacksaw on a flat surface. A ragged cut leaves a gap where water can start again.
  1. Press the new seal onto the glass. Start at one end and work down the length, seating the channel fully as you go. It should snap or slide on snugly without forcing, a sign the profile is correct.
  1. Test with water. Run the shower and watch the bottom corners and the curb closely. Reseating a corner that pops loose now is much easier than dealing with a wet floor later.

Jacksonville Climate Considerations

Northeast Florida summers push high humidity into every bathroom, and a shower with a weak or missing exhaust fan stays damp for hours after use. That slow drying time breaks down vinyl seals faster here than in a drier climate, and it also feeds mildew along the door track even when the seal is intact. In parts of Duval County with harder water, mineral scale builds up inside the sweep's channel, which stiffens the vinyl and kills the seal well before it looks worn. A leaking sweep is not just a nuisance puddle. Water running down the outside of the curb, day after day, eventually reaches the subfloor and framing around the shower, and that kind of rot does not announce itself until the floor feels soft or a stain shows up on the ceiling below.

Not every leak is the door's fault. If water is coming up through the tile floor, staining a ceiling below a second-floor bathroom, or showing up as moisture at the base of walls outside the shower footprint, that points to a failed pan liner or bad waterproofing, not a worn seal. Press a finger against the grout near the drain after a shower. If it feels spongy or the tile flexes, the pan or subfloor is the real problem, and new vinyl on the door will not fix it. That points to the pan replacement and permit path above, not a weekend hardware swap.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my shower door leak is the seal or the pan?
Watch where the water shows up. If it stays limited to the area right outside the door during or after a shower, and the grout and tile floor stay dry and firm, the seal is the likely cause. If you find moisture at the base of walls outside the shower footprint, a soft or spongy feel in the tile near the drain, or staining on a ceiling below, that points to the pan or waterproofing, and a new sweep will not resolve it.
Can I use any universal shower door sweep, or does it need to match exactly?
It needs to match your door's channel width and wing profile closely. A universal sweep sold as one-size-fits-all often seats loosely, which lets it pop off the glass or leave gaps at the corners. Bringing the old piece, or clear measurements and a photo, to a glass supplier is the most reliable way to get a proper match.
Should I caulk the curb instead of replacing the seals?
If the seals and sweep are still flexible and seated tightly but you are seeing water at the bottom corners or along the curb, the caulk joint between the glass frame and the curb or tile has likely failed, and recaulking is the right fix. If the vinyl is cracked, shrunk or stiff, replace the seal first, since fresh caulk will not compensate for a seal that no longer contacts the glass.
